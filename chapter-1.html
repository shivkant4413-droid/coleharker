Game Over

The bug report was seventy-one words long, and every one of them was going to get ignored.

Steps to reproduce: Load into Sector 4. Equip the Ashfall Cloak. Sprint-jump off the collapsed bridge. Player clips through world geometry and falls into the void shader, which is not supposed to render, but does, forever, at four frames per second, like God's own screensaver.

Kade Vance had filed one hundred and eighty-three of these since March. He knew, with the calm certainty of a man who has stopped expecting anything good to happen to him, that this one would sit in the backlog next to the other one hundred and eighty-two, tagged P3 — Won't Fix This Sprint, until the game shipped broken and three million people found the same hole he'd found for free.

"Severity: high," he muttered, typing it anyway. "Priority: nobody cares."

The office at 3:12 a.m. had the quality of light that only exists in crunch, every monitor at full brightness, every human at zero. Somewhere behind him, Priya was asleep sitting up, chin on her chest, controller still twitching in her lap like she was fighting monsters in her dreams that she couldn't quite manage to fight in real life. The build kept crashing. The coffee machine had died around midnight and nobody had the will to mourn it.

Kade cracked open his fourth energy drink of the shift. The can hissed like it agreed with him about the state of his life.

He was twenty-eight years old. He had a degree he wasn't using, a lease he couldn't really afford, and a job title, Senior QA Analyst, that meant, in practice, that he was paid to notice everything wrong with a thing that everyone else got

to pretend was finished. He was good at it. He was good at nothing else. He'd tried, a few times, to be good at other things, a relationship, a hobby, a sleep schedule, and every one of them had come back to him tagged the same way his bug reports did. Won't fix. Not this sprint. Maybe never.

"You're still here," Priya mumbled, not opening her eyes.

"Someone has to hold the line against the void shader."

"Go home, Kade."

"I am home. This chair has better back support than my bed."

That, unfortunately, was true. He typed the last line of the report — Recommend: fix before launch, this will end up on every clip channel on day one, and hit submit, and felt the hollow satisfaction of doing his job well in a system that had already decided not to listen to him.

His phone buzzed on the desk. He glanced at it.

Mom: are you eating? you sounded tired sunday

It was from four days ago. He'd seen it. He'd meant to answer. He'd meant to answer the one before that, too, and the one about her knee, and the one with the photo of the neighbor's new dog. There were eleven unread messages under her name. He'd been meaning to answer all of them since August.

He picked up the phone. He typed yeah mom im fine, just crunch, ill call this weekend. He looked at it. It was the same thing he'd sent her in June. And March. He was pretty sure it was word for word.

He didn't send it. He put the phone face down on the desk, the way you put down something you'll deal with later, and told himself he'd call her on Saturday. Properly. For an hour. He'd ask about her knee.

"You know what I used to want to do?" he said to Priya's sleeping back, because it was 3 a.m. and there was nobody awake to hear it. "Before this. I used to want to make them. Games. I had a notebook. I had a whole world in it. Systems,

magic, a map. It was going to be the one game where nobody could find an exploit, because I'd have found them all first."

Priya snored softly.

"Yeah," Kade said. "That's about what my manager said too."

The notebook was still in his apartment somewhere, under a pile of unopened mail. He hadn't looked at it in four years.

His vision did something strange right about then. A gray fizz crawled in from the edges, like a stream buffering. He put it down to four energy drinks and no sleep since Tuesday.

It was not four energy drinks and no sleep since Tuesday.

The last thing Kade Vance saw on Earth was his own bug report, sitting in the queue, status already quietly flipped to Closed — Won't Fix by some manager who hadn't read past the first line. The last thing he thought was, of course. And then his chest did something it had apparently been trying to warn him about for a while, a white, cracking pain, an alarm going off in a language his body had never bothered to teach him, and the monitor's light smeared sideways, and the office noise dropped out like someone had pulled the audio cable, and everything went the flat, matte black of a screen that has simply stopped receiving signal.

No loading bar. No prompt. Just: nothing.

Which was, Kade would think much later, a genuinely unacceptable way to handle a fatal event. At minimum you owed the player a screen. Game Over. Something. Anything to confirm the state had actually changed, instead of just, stopping.

◆ ◆ ◆

He came back online to noise.

Not sound, more like the feeling of sound, a pressure behind his ears, before the actual sensory data caught up and started reporting: wet stone under his cheek, the reek of rot

and old rain, a bell tolling somewhere too many times, and, underneath all of it, cold.

Kade opened his eyes onto a narrow alley between two buildings made of gray brick and worse decisions, sky the color of a badly-graded cutscene, and the immediate, overriding thought was not where am I or am I dead or even what happened to my body.

It was: why is there a health bar.

Floating in the corner of his vision, not on a screen, not projected on anything, just there, the way a headache is there, sat a thin red bar, mostly full, with a number beside it he didn't recognize as belonging to anyone. Under it, a smaller blue bar. Under that, a smaller yellow one. All three had the exact ugly drop-shadow of a placeholder UI element nobody had gotten around to polishing.

He sat up. The alley spun once, politely, and settled.

"Okay," Kade said out loud, to nobody, in a voice that was almost his but not quite, a little higher, a little younger, definitely not the voice of a man who'd been drinking energy drinks since he was nineteen. "Okay. So either I'm having the world's most detailed stress dream, or —"

Text bloomed across the middle distance, pale and slightly transparent, the way subtitles look when a projector's bulb is dying.

[ Welcome, unregistered soul. ]

Kade stared at it.

Six months of live-service crunch had trained a very specific reflex into him, and that reflex fired now, faster than fear, faster than wonder, faster than the extremely reasonable question of whether he was alive.

"'Unregistered,'" he repeated. He almost laughed, a short, ugly bark of a sound that hurt his throat, like the throat wasn't used to laughing yet. "Of course I am. Of course the tutorial's broken. Of course I died and woke up in the one build that shipped without onboarding."

He reached out, an old habit, the muscle memory of a thousand playtests, and tried to swipe the message away like a toast notification.

His fingers passed through nothing. But the message didn't dismiss. Instead, something else opened underneath it, a second panel, slower, like it had been waiting for that gesture, that input, and nobody had ever bothered to give it.

[ Unauthorized menu access detected. ]

[ Interface: DEBUG. ]

[ Warning: this interface is not visible to other users. ]

Kade went very still.

Not because he was scared, though, in fairness, he probably should have been. Because for the first time since a white pain had cracked open his chest in an office three million miles and possibly one universe away, something in front of him made sense. It was a menu. It had labels. It had a system underneath it, doing what systems do: responding to input, badly, in a way its designers clearly hadn't intended and definitely hadn't tested.

"There it is," Kade said softly, and, cold stone under him and a stranger's body around him, he almost smiled. "There's the bug."
